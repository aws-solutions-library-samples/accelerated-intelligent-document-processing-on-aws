GenAIIDP Configuration Guidance — Empirical Guidance for Document Extraction at Scale

Section titled “GenAIIDP Configuration Guidance — Empirical Guidance for Document Extraction at Scale”

Release: v0.6.13 · Region: us-west-2 · Stack: IDPUpg0612to0613 (created from the published idp-main_0.6.12.yaml and upgraded in place to the published idp-main_0.6.13.yaml; the v0.6.13 record in release-validation carries the stack’s provenance) Models: extraction Claude Sonnet 4.6 — the cross-version control, not the shipped default — in §2–§4 and §7; the extraction, classification and confidence models are varied in §5 · classification Nova 2 Lite (the shipped default) · confidence Nova Lite (the shipped default) · summarization disabled (unscored) Pricing: config_library/pricing.yaml (sha256 44a79aa06c15…, unchanged since the v0.6.12 edition; rates as of 2026-10; intro pricing may apply) Measured: 2026-10-31, 04:17 → 12:03 UTC (run launch times), in one session, on one stack — 3,368 scored rows across 31 result sets (2,608 synthetic runs and 760 reference-corpus documents, $2,077.09 by the rows’ own cost) plus a classification-variant benchmark on a 20-packet labelled corpus, which is new in this edition.

⚠️ Four models’ cost figures are not measurements of what the price list charges, in two opposite directions. pricing.yaml holds one flat rate per model, and that is wrong for four of the swept models:

  • Claude Haiku 5.5 is billed 5× on every token type above 100,000 input tokens, and that threshold is crossed in practice here. Its figures are lower bounds.
  • GPT-6 Astra moves to a long-context band above 272,000 input tokens. Its figures are lower bounds.
  • Claude Sonnet 5 and Sonnet 5 :1m, the shipped default and its 1M twin, are priced in pricing.yaml at Sonnet 4.6’s rates, which is 1.5× the published Sonnet 5 price on every token type ($3.30/$16.50 per MTok against $2.20/$11.00, us./eu.). The file says so and leaves it for a separate change. So the Bedrock-token part of every Sonnet 5 figure is overstated, and those figures are upper bounds.
  • Claude Sonnet 5.5 is priced at its published rates, which are identical to Sonnet 5’s on input and output ($2.20/$11.00) and half on cache reads. Against the inflated Sonnet 5 rows a report therefore shows Sonnet 5.5 as about a third cheaper than Sonnet 5 when the price list says they are nearly the same. Compare those two from the AWS price list, never from here.

Where this page compares one of these models on cost it states two dollar figures from the same grid and a one-sided bound over them (“at least X% less”) in the direction the bias allows, which is what a bound supports and a ratio is not. Every other arm has a single flat rate and its cost figure is a true measurement. See each model’s row in Benchmarking.

Abstract

Section titled “Abstract”

We benchmark the GenAI IDP accelerator across a controlled matrix of configuration options (OCR backend, extraction mode, assessment mode, the v0.7 feature arms, the extraction, classification and confidence models, and — new in this edition — the classification granularity, modality and strategy) and document types and sizes (synthetic documents with exact ground truth, plus three real labelled corpora). We quantify seven dimensions per configuration: success/failure, list completeness, per-row field accuracy, confidence calibration, latency, token use, and cost.

Headline results at v0.6.13:

  1. Both Opus models are now at ceiling on the whole extraction grid, and Opus 5.5 is the cheaper of the two. On the 19-cell × 7-document grid Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Opus 5 each completed 133 of 133 runs at recall 1.0000 and cell accuracy 1.0000 over 83,790 cells_compared, the grid’s full denominator — for $107.67 against $128.52, 16% less for the same result, a ratio that can be quoted because both are flat-rate. Opus 5.5 is also the faster of the two (244 s against 270 s per document). GPT-6 Astra, the most expensive arm, is at recall 0.9925 and the lowest cell accuracy of the grid, 0.9735 over 82,188 cells, for $188.81, which is a lower bound — so Opus 5.5 is at least 43% less than Astra and more accurate (§5.1, §5.5).
  2. The shipped default truncates the 17-page document in simple mode more often than it did, and still reports success. Sonnet 5’s grid recall is 0.9307 over 69,042 cells, the second-lowest of the seven arms, and all of the shortfall is one document: on the 800-row large_narrow in simple mode it returned all 800 rows on 3 of 13 runs and 43 to 141 rows on the other 10, each COMPLETED (v0.6.12: a mean of ~454 of 800). In advanced mode the same model returned 800 of 800 on 6 of 6. The simple-mode behaviour is bimodal at this size — §5.2’s astravalue measures the same model and document at 4 complete runs of 5 — and this grid has one draw per cell, so read the movement from v0.6.12 as variation between draws rather than as a release effect. The rule from the last two editions holds and is stronger: use advanced mode above ~400 rows (§5.5, §3).
  3. On a real labelled corpus simple mode still beats advanced at about half the cost. On RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, all 19 cells, scored by the product’s own evaluation) eleven simple-mode cells score 0.803–0.811 weighted accuracy and five of the six advanced cells fall below every one of them, at 0.775–0.786. The matched pair costs $0.1437 simple against $0.3287 advanced. The sixth advanced cell, core-tt-adv-int, reaches the simple band at 0.805 for $0.4911 per document. These rows carry no cells_compared: a reference document’s truth lives on the stack, so the metric is weighted_accuracy over 20 documents per cell (§2c).
  4. 🚨 Claude Sonnet 5.5 still loses most of a 400-row list (#1494). 16 of its 133 grid runs return between 0 and 162 rows of 400 — twelve return exactly 10 — and every one is a 400-row document. Per-document recall is 0.6900 on med_narrow (10,480 cells), 0.7108 on wide_400 (10,804) and 0.7947 on manylists_400 (12,080), against 1.0000 on every document of 100 rows or fewer and on the 800-row one. 6 of the 16 carry no completeness issue at all, and that is structural: on the 8-column wide_400 and on Textract LAYOUT text there is no exact-width OCR table to check a row count against, and 10 rows is under the 30-row floor below which even the new extraction_row_count_unverified report stays silent (§5.1).
  5. The confidence pass no longer discards a complete large extraction — it now returns it with most of the confidence missing. In the scaling study the 1,200- and 1,600-row simple-mode documents, which failed at v0.6.12 after every row had been extracted, now complete on 6 of 6 runs with every row at cell accuracy 1.000 — and with assessment_incomplete on all six, because the confidence pass now stops at its time budget (#1488) and scores only 33–38% of the rows at 1,200 and 19–23% at 1,600. Across the whole refresh the large_narrow assessment failure fell from 15 of 190 simple-mode runs to 2 of 190, both memory kills (#1678). Advanced mode holds recall 1.000 and full confidence coverage at every size from 25 to 3,200 rows (§3).
  6. Classification granularity, modality and strategy have their first measurement, and packet granularity places boundaries far better than the default. On 20 DocSplit packets with Nova 2 Lite, over the 15 packets every variant scored, the shipped page-granularity default places section boundaries correctly at 0.446 and the four packet-level variants at 0.711–0.821, at 23–65% of its classification cost per page. packet + images only has the best page class accuracy, 0.909 against the default’s 0.851. ⚠️ But every packet variant that sends page images failed the same three 18–20-page packets with Input is too long for requested model (#1676), and text-only page granularity failed two packets on a page with no OCR text (#1677). Twenty documents is a small sample, so read this as direction and failure modes rather than as a ranking (§5.6).
  7. Neither premium knob on the other two model axes earns its price. Upgrading the classification model buys nothing measurable: every arm produces downstream recall 1.0000 over the same 83,790 cells, the Nova 2 Lite default and Haiku 5.5 get the section boundary right on 133 of 133 runs and Sonnet 5 on 131 of 133, and the grid bill rises from $89.10 to $113.59 under Sonnet 5. Sonnet 5 as the confidence model is a far more conservative scorer, not a more accurate one: about 285 of 119,968 confidence leaves below 0.9 against the default’s 0 of 119,921, for $199.82 against $89.97, with identical cell accuracy (0.9997 over 83,790 cells in all three arms). The two Sonnet 5 premiums are upper bounds, because pricing.yaml overstates Sonnet 5 (§5.3, §5.4).
  8. Both standing list-loss hazards stay closed. integrated confidence with simple extraction is recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 on 3 of 3 repeats, at the same cost to the cent as separate ($0.2053 against $0.2052), and the advanced-mode tool-decline list loss is 0 of 6 at the shipped default (§2.1). 131 of the 133 grid runs produced exactly one section; the two exceptions split the same 8-column 400-row statement in two (288 + 112 rows). They are enforce-warn and force-off, which resolve to exactly the same configuration as core-tt-simple-sep and split-llm, and those two did not split — so this is one classifier draw in four going the other way, not a configuration effect.
  9. Bedrock-LLM OCR has a readable accuracy figure again, and it is still the one backend that gets values wrong. With identifiers repaired it returns every row — recall 1.000 over 4,410 cells, the same denominator as every other cell — at cell accuracy 0.9950, the only cell of the nineteen below 1.000, and 10 of 14 scalar fields right where every other cell gets 12 to 14. It is also the cheapest cell, at $0.3465 per document. Do not use it where identifiers or amounts matter (§2, finding 4).
  10. The cheap end completes everything and returns half the rows. Neither Nova model can finish the agentic path (#895), so both ran the smaller simplegrid matrix, where they completed 63 of 63 runs at mean recall 0.5054 (Nova Pro) and 0.4964 (Nova Lite). Nova Pro’s 0.9324 cell accuracy is over 7,764 cells against the same matrix’s Sonnet 4.6 control at 39,690 (§5.1).

What changed between releases is tracked separately in the Release Audit Trail — this paper focuses on choosing a configuration at the current release.

1. Methodology (summary)

Section titled “1. Methodology (summary)”

See benchmarks/matrices/METHODOLOGY.md for the full protocol. In brief:

  • Synthetic corpus (exact GT): generated bank statements whose every transaction row carries a unique SEQnnnnn tag, so completeness and accuracy are measured exactly, and size, row width, list count, text length and OCR noise are controlled variables.
  • Reference corpora (real, labelled): RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, one class) and OmniAI-OCR-Benchmark (20 documents, nine classes), run as Test Studio test sets and scored by the product’s own evaluation against their labels. Both were run for this release (core suite), at the control model only.
  • Classification corpus (real, labelled per page): the first 20 packets of DocSplit-Poly-Seq (RVL-CDIP-derived, 13 classes, 298 pages), run through benchmarks/harness/run_classification_bench.py and scored per page and per section against its labels (§5.6).
  • Config matrix: 19 curated core cells — the OCR × mode × assessment decision space plus the v0.7 feature arms (enforcement, forcing, schema restatement, section splitting) — a two-cell scaling series, a repeated-measures cost suite, the two hazard re-verification suites (intconf, advverify), the feature A/B suites for the knobs named in the scope table, and the model sweeps. Control arms live in a separate control_cells: block and are excluded from core_cells.
  • Scoring is resolver-free (reads S3 + DynamoDB metering directly); costs priced from pricing.yaml; calibration from explainability_info confidence leaves.

2. Configuration matrix (19 configurations, 7 synthetic documents with exact ground truth)

Section titled “2. Configuration matrix (19 configurations, 7 synthetic documents with exact ground truth)”

Mean over 7 bank-statement (transaction-list) documents spanning 5 → 800 rows and varying row width, list count and description length (tiny_form, small_narrow, med_narrow, large_narrow, wide_400, manylists_400, longdesc_100). recall = distinct ground-truth rows recovered ÷ total (exact, via SEQ tags); cell acc = per-row typed value match, keyed by SEQ tag, reported beside the cells_compared it was computed over. Extraction model Sonnet 4.6, the cross-version control — the core suite carries no model override, so §2 is a cross-configuration result at one model, and the shipped default’s own grid is §5.1’s. 133 runs, 0 failures, and 131 of the 133 produced exactly one section; the two exceptions are the same 400-row document split in two, finding 6.

All nineteen cells are at recall 1.000 over 4,410 cells_compared each, on all 7 documents — including the 800-row, 17-page one — and eighteen are also at cell accuracy 1.000. How simple mode behaves as documents grow

Section titled “How simple mode behaves as documents grow”

The simple-mode column above is not a gradient. It is three distinct behaviours with two sharp boundaries, and they want different responses from a deployment:

  1. 25 → 800 rows / 1 → 17 pages: it completes, with everything. COMPLETED, recall 1.000, cell accuracy 1.000, every row confidence-scored. At 800 rows the confidence pass is already the larger part of the bill — $0.70 of $1.64 per document, against $0.04 for the same pass in advanced mode — and of the wall-clock: 819 s against 196 s at 400 rows. The batch-shrinking ladder converges there (assessment_recovered_with_retries on all three runs, 3–11 rows recovered, 0 unrecoverable).
  2. ⚠️ 1,200 and 1,600 rows / 25 and 33 pages: every row is extracted and returned, and most of them have no confidence. All six runs report COMPLETED, recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 over 2,400 and 3,200 cells_compared, validated clean. And all six carry assessment_incomplete at error severity, with deadline_reached: the confidence pass scored 401–461 of 1,200 rows and 309–372 of 1,600, then stopped to stay inside the Lambda’s time budget, so the remaining 739–1,291 rows per document come back with confidence: null and a per-row reason naming the time budget. This is the #1488 fix working as designed. At v0.6.12 both of these sizes failed after a complete extraction, because the pass was killed by its Lambda and no degrade path ran. Now the document arrives, complete, with a reported gap — which is the better outcome, and which a consumer that gates review on confidence has to handle, because an unscored row raises no confidence alert.
  3. 3,200 rows / 66 pages: refused before extraction inference, which is correct. FAILED in about 50 s having returned 0 rows and 0 sections, with no extraction tokens billed at all — the spend is the OCR and classification that had already run. The request is too long for one call; benchmarks/matrices/config_matrix.yaml records this size as an expected rejection rather than a regression, and §5.2’s astracap reproduces it 6 times over, three times on Sonnet 5 and three on GPT-6 Astra. Refusing is the right outcome: nothing is attempted and nothing wrong is returned.

⚠️ So in regime 2 neither status nor completeness_recall tells you what is missing — conf_coverage and the assessment_incomplete issue do. A reader who ranks by recall or by completion rate concludes simple mode is flawless at 1,600 rows, and on the extraction it is. What is missing is the confidence on up to four rows in five.

⚠️ No per-page input-token budget is published for the refused size. A refused document bills no extraction tokens, so there is no record of the request. What the suite does measure is the simple-mode extraction input on the six sizes that completed — 2,552, 7,958, 26,616, 51,433, 76,482 and 101,733 tokens at 1, 3, 9, 17, 25 and 33 pages, about 3,100 tokens per page over the 3–33-page span, with the 1-page point below that line because fixed overhead dominates it. Extrapolating it to 66 pages would produce a number this suite does not support, so none is given.

The product mechanism behind regime 3 is unchanged and is documented rather than measured here. Simple mode’s pre-flight estimate logs and sends; what refuses an oversized request is Bedrock, on the size of the request its page images make, and #994 relieved it by clamping every image in a many-image request to 2,000 px per side. Two separate Bedrock limits bind: a stricter per-image dimension cap once a request carries more than 20 image blocks, and a cap on the total request payload. It is the payload limit that produces ExtractionInputTooLarge, because Bedrock reports an oversized payload with the same “Input is too long for requested model” wording it uses for a context overflow; the per-image dimension rejection has its own distinct wording and is matched separately (see the note in idp_common/utils/bedrock_utils.py). The clamp cuts pixel area, so it relieves both.

The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)

Section titled “The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)”

extraction.row_shortfall_action is the knob that lets an observed row shortfall decide the outcome, added at 0.6.10 for the truncated-but-COMPLETED shape — a run that returns a fraction of a list and reports success, which §5.1 measures on four extraction models at 17 pages and on Sonnet 5.5 at 9. Nothing in this suite exercises it: no run here lost a row, so extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate never fired. The knob is described here because it is the control a reader reaches for after reading about truncation, not because this table measures it.

The extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate detection is unchanged — the rows extracted against the rows in the section’s OCR tables of the same width, a floor of 30 and a “fewer than half” ratio — and under fail the partial rows and the diagnosis are written before the section fails, so a truncated run reports FAILED with a message naming the rows extracted, the OCR estimate and the remedy. What is new in this release is the case where that check cannot run: when the matched evidence is under 30 rows while 30 or more rows came back, the section now carries extraction_row_count_unverified (info) instead of silence (#1489). It reports the absence of evidence and never a shortfall. ⚠️ Its floor cuts both ways: a list truncated to fewer than 30 rows on a document without exact-width tables is reported by neither issue, which is exactly the shape of Sonnet 5.5’s 10-row runs on wide_400 in §5.1.

The default is warn. The reason is the check’s evidence, not caution: matched tables are summed over the whole section on width alone, and a 2- or 3-property array modelling an entity group is structurally identical to one modelling table rows. On the default preset a completely correct extraction of Bank-Statement.account_summary (2 properties, 5 rows) scores 0.13, because a monthly statement’s 31-row two-column Daily Balance table is counted as evidence about it. Nine such fields ship in the config library, so fail as a default would fail correct extractions. Extraction and confidence lists the shapes to check before turning it on.

Two notes on the threshold. It is not a new number: the failure fires exactly where the warning already fired, so no second threshold was chosen to make these cells come out right. And across the 3,631 recorded benchmark runs that reach the check’s population, non-zero recall is strongly bimodal — 65 runs below 0.5, 3,368 at ~1.000, and one single run anywhere in [0.3, 0.5) — so the outcome is insensitive to the ratio’s exact value within that band. Of those 65, three are Advanced-mode runs (of 1,231 Advanced runs in the population) and all three lost real rows, though only one lost them as a clean stop-early cut: the other two have truncation_prefix = 0, meaning the loss is scattered through the list rather than a prefix. What none of this establishes is the false-failure rate against real corpora: the 65 are all runs that genuinely lost more than half their rows, and the group-shaped-array problem above was found by reading the shipped schemas, not measured on a document set.

Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it

Section titled “Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it”

On v0.6.7 simple mode reported “complete” at 1,200 rows and recovered 0.72–0.79 of the rows at 3,200 — and that completeness was an artefact of a different behaviour: the classifier split every large document into 6–18 sections, each small enough to extract. With #726/#817 every document is one section and that scaffolding is gone. Three things follow:

  1. The simple-mode envelope for a complete result on this corpus is 800 rows / 17 pages, and the 800-row point is the one to hold loosely. At the control model it completes with all 800 rows and full confidence on 3 of 3 runs here and on all 19 of §2’s cells — but §5.1 measures the same 17-page document in simple mode returning between 0 and 141 of 800 rows on some runs of four of the seven extraction models while reporting COMPLETED, the shipped default among them (10 of its 13 runs). The single long response is what varies, and which side of the limit a run lands on moves with the model and with the input text. Treat ~400 rows / ~10 pages as the comfortable simple-mode envelope and 800 as the edge of it; use advanced mode above that.
  2. Above the envelope, simple mode returns the rows without the confidence, then stops being available. At 1,200 and 1,600 rows the extraction is complete and up to 81% of the rows are unscored (regime 2); at 3,200 rows the request is refused outright (regime 3). Neither is improved by tuning the extraction: the remedy at every size above the envelope is advanced mode, which returns every row with confidence at all seven sizes. The assessment_incomplete message names two other remedies — more Lambda time, or less work per section — and on this evidence they treat the confidence half only.
  3. ⚠️ No single column summarises a simple-mode run at size. COMPLETED is not evidence of completeness — §5.1’s truncated-but-completed runs are the proof — and recall 1.000 is not evidence that the document is review-ready, because at 1,200 and 1,600 rows most rows are unscored. Read status, completeness_recall, cells_compared and conf_coverage together.

Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow

Section titled “Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow”

Advanced (agentic sharding) holds recall 1.000, cell accuracy 1.000 and full confidence coverage from 25 through 3,200 rows / 66 pages at the control model, on 21 of 21 runs, on denominators from 50 to 6,400 cells_compared per run — and, in the premium study (§5.2), at Sonnet 5 and GPT-6 Astra too.

The practical limits are cost and wall-clock, and at the control model both stay modest: $4.6034 and 602 s at 3,200 rows (range $4.39–4.99, 465–747 s), against $0.1023 and 38 s at 25 rows. Cost is close to linear in rows from 400 upward — $0.00145, $0.00149, $0.00145, $0.00146 and $0.00144 per row at 400, 800, 1,200, 1,600 and 3,200 rows — so there is no cost cliff at the top of this series; the 25-row point is the outlier, at $0.00409 per row, because the fixed per-document overhead dominates a one-page document.

From 800 rows upward advanced mode is the cheaper mode as well as the complete one: $1.1957 against simple’s $1.6437 at 800 rows, $1.7410 against $2.5168 at 1,200 and $2.3357 against $3.0151 at 1,600 — and simple mode at those two sizes buys a third or less of the confidence advanced mode delivers in full. The difference is almost all the confidence pass, which costs $0.04–0.08 per document in advanced mode across that range and $0.70–1.23 in simple mode. For the shipped default at the 3,200-row size, §5.2’s astracap measures Sonnet 5 at $4.95–5.58 over three repeats, 434 s mean — and those Sonnet 5 figures are upper bounds (see the header).

Turn it off for shard headroom, not for dollars. The input saving is genuine but cheap: it is almost entirely cache reads, so 9,181 fewer input-side tokens is worth about $0.007 — while 2,347 more output tokens cost about $0.039. Those two figures reconcile the measured extraction-cost difference (+$0.0319/doc) to the cent. ⚠️ The release grids do not corroborate the sign. At one draw per document they put restate-off below restate-on in both of the last two editions — $0.6164 against $0.6188 here, $0.6142 against $0.6292 at v0.6.12 — a 0.4% and a 2.4% difference inside the agentic spread §4 measures. So the cost claim rests on the dedicated A/B alone, which itself does not reach significance on cost. Since #775 the reclaimed tokens are real shard budget, so headroom is the reason to use this knob; whether it moves a shard count still depends on the document sitting near a boundary.

⚠️ The output-token increase is the part to watch, and it is bimodal rather than uniform. On manylists_400 every restate-on run emitted 13,406–13,590 output tokens, while five of fifteen restate-off runs emitted 20,201–20,368 — a mode the on arm never entered (Fisher p = 0.042). The remaining ten sat only 100–250 tokens above the on arm. So the risk of turning it off is not a slightly chattier agent every time; it is a roughly one-in-three chance of a run that emits half again as much. Measured on one model and one document shape.
