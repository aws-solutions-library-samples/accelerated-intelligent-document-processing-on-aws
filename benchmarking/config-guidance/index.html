Section titled “GenAIIDP Configuration Guidance — Empirical Guidance for Document Extraction at Scale”

Release: v0.6.12 · Region: us-west-2 · Stack: IDPUpg0611to0612 (created from the published idp-main_0.6.11.yaml and upgraded in place to the published idp-main_0.6.12.yaml; the v0.6.12 record in release-validation carries the stack’s provenance and the failure census these runs contributed to) Models: extraction Claude Sonnet 4.6 — the cross-version control, not the shipped default — in §2–§4 and §7; the extraction, classification and confidence models are varied in §5 · classification Nova 2 Lite (the shipped default) · confidence Nova Lite (the shipped default) · summarization disabled (unscored) Pricing: config_library/pricing.yaml (sha256 44a79aa06c15…; rates as of 2026-10; intro pricing may apply) Measured: 2026-10-17 23:37 → 2026-10-18 06:22 UTC, in one session, on one stack — 3,248 scored rows from 2,488 runs across 29 result sets, including the first measurement of the two reference corpora since the v0.6.8 edition and the first of any kind for Claude Sonnet 5.5.

⚠️ Three cost figures in §5 are lower bounds, not measurements, and no cost ratio is published for any of the three models. Where §5 does compare one of them on cost, it states two dollar figures from the same grid and a one-sided bound over them (“at least X% less”) — which is what a lower bound supports and a ratio is not. pricing.yaml holds one flat rate per model and cannot express a usage-dependent band, and three swept models need one:

  • Claude Haiku 5.5 is billed 5× on every token type above 100,000 input tokens, and that threshold is crossed in practice here.
  • GPT-6 Astra moves to a long-context band above 272,000 input tokens.
  • Claude Sonnet 5.5 is a different problem with the same consequence: its published per-token input and output prices are identical to Sonnet 5’s ($2.20/$11.00 per MTok, us./eu.), differing only on cache reads ($0.11 vs $0.22) — but the Sonnet 5 rows in pricing.yaml carry Sonnet 4.6’s rates by a separately-tracked decision, so a report shows Sonnet 5.5 as roughly a third cheaper than Sonnet 5 when the price list says they are the same. Compare those two from the AWS price list, never from here.

Every other arm in the sweep has a single flat rate and its cost figure is a true measurement. See each model’s row in Benchmarking.

Abstract

Section titled “Abstract”

We benchmark the GenAI IDP accelerator across a controlled matrix of configuration options (OCR backend, extraction mode, assessment mode, the v0.7 feature arms, and — new in this edition — the extraction, classification and confidence models) and document types and sizes (synthetic documents with exact ground truth, plus two real labelled corpora). We quantify seven dimensions per configuration: success/failure, list completeness, per-row field accuracy, confidence calibration, latency, token use, and cost.

Headline results at v0.6.12:

  1. The model axis is measured in full for the first time, and Claude Opus 5.5 tops it. All nine swept extraction models ran on this one release, as did all three arms of both the classification and the confidence axis. On the 19-cell × 7-document grid Opus 5.5 is the only arm at both perfect recall and perfect cell accuracy — 1.0000 and 1.0000 over 83,790 cells_compared, the largest denominator of any arm — for $101.67 against GPT-6 Astra’s $209.28 over the same grid. So the most expensive arm in the sweep is matched on recall and beaten on cell accuracy (0.9737 over 83,788 cells) by one costing at least 51% less, a bound rather than a ratio because Astra’s figure is itself a lower bound. What the completeness costs is wall-clock: Opus 5.5 averages 376 s per document against the next-slowest arm’s 221 s, a gap concentrated in simple mode on the 17-page document and absent in advanced mode (§5.1, §5.5).
  2. On a real labelled corpus simple mode beats advanced at about half the cost — and the synthetic grid cannot see it. On RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, all 19 cells, scored by the product’s own evaluation) the eleven highest-scoring cells are all simple mode, at 0.8029–0.8119 weighted accuracy, and every one of the six advanced cells falls below them, at 0.7584–0.7911. The matched pair costs $0.1423 simple against $0.2757 advanced. The two simple cells that do not clear the advanced band are the two with a known handicap — forced tool use (0.7892) and Bedrock-LLM OCR (0.7661). The exact-ground-truth synthetic grid scores 1.000 on recall and cell accuracy almost everywhere and so ranks nothing, which is why the reference corpora earn their run cost. These rows carry no cells_compared: a reference document’s truth lives on the stack, so the metric is weighted_accuracy over 20 documents per cell (§2c).
  3. 🚨 Claude Sonnet 5.5 loses nearly the whole list on 400-row documents (#1494). 13 of its 133 grid runs return 2 or 10 rows of 400 — four at recall 0.005, nine at 0.025 — and every one of the 13 is a 400-row document; on the four at 0.005 the field came back as a prose refusal rather than a short list, so zero rows were returned and the 2 is the scorer counting the two row identifiers the refusal sentence quotes (#1505). Per-document recall is 0.6366 on med_narrow (9,660 cells) and 0.7434 on manylists_400 (11,300), against 1.0000 on every document of 100 rows or fewer and on the 800-row one. Its grid cell accuracy of 1.0000 is computed on the rows it chose to return, over 73,570 cells against the grid’s largest 83,790 — so the row reads “cheap and perfect” only if the denominator is ignored. On a 400-row list it is the worst arm in the table (§5.1).
  4. Mode, not model, decides an 800-row document — and simple mode still reports success on a truncated list. On the 17-page, 800-row document all seven coresynth arms return 800 of 800 rows in advanced mode: 6 of 6 runs each, recall 1.0000 and cell accuracy 1.0000 over 9,600 cells per arm, no exceptions. In simple mode the same seven split. Three return everything (20,800 cells), Opus 5 returns ~738 of 800 (19,200), and Sonnet 5, Sonnet 5 :1m and Haiku 5.5 return ~454, ~401 and ~351 of 800 while reaching COMPLETED in 13 of 13 cells each — with cell accuracy 0.9985–1.0000 because it is computed on the rows they returned, over 9,136 to 11,814 cells. Nothing downstream flags it. Rank by completeness, never by completion rate, and never by an accuracy figure without its denominator (§5.5, §3).
  5. Advanced mode holds the 3,200-row / 66-page ceiling; widening the shard breaks it silently. At default shards both models measured at that size returned 3,200 of 3,200 rows at cell accuracy 1.0000 over 12,800 cells each — Sonnet 5 at $5.80 and $5.82, Astra at $18.39 and $16.25, so Sonnet 5 is at least 66% less — while simple mode refuses the same document up front with ExtractionInputTooLarge, which is the correct behaviour. The 25-page “wide” shard setting is the one that fails: all four runs reached COMPLETED and returned 1,982 to 3,200 of 3,200 rows, at cell accuracy 1.0000 on denominators 19% and 30% smaller, and on Sonnet 5 at a higher price than the default shard. Leave shard_pages at its default at this size (§5.2, §6).
  6. Neither premium knob on the other two model axes earns its price. Upgrading the classification model buys nothing measurable and costs 29% more: the section boundary is right on 132 of 133 runs under the Nova 2 Lite default and 132 of 133 under Sonnet 5, downstream recall is identical to four decimal places (0.9982 both) and cell accuracy marginally worse (0.9991 against 0.9995) on denominators within 0.2% of each other, while the grid bill goes from $88.15 to $113.81. Sonnet 5 as the confidence model is not an accuracy upgrade either — it is a far more conservative scorer, flagging about 283 of 119,980 confidence leaves below 0.9 against the default’s 8 of 119,943, for $199.95 against $91.20 over the same grid. Its cell accuracy (0.9995 over 83,562 cells) is a rounding difference from the default’s (0.9991 over 83,396). That conservatism is worth paying for only where the corpus contains wrong values, and §5.4 measures the one grid cell that does (§5.3, §5.4).
  7. Both standing list-loss hazards are closed, and the over-splitting that shaped the v0.6.7 edition is gone. integrated confidence with simple extraction — 0.294 at v0.6.5, 0.936 at v0.6.7 — is recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 on 8 of 8 repeats over 1,600 cells, at the same cost to the cent as separate ($0.207 against $0.208), because list-bearing classes are now routed to a separate pass automatically (#795). The advanced-mode tool-decline list loss is 0 of 8, likewise 1.000 and 1.000 over 1,600 cells (§2.1). And 132 of the 133 grid runs produced exactly one section, which is the truth — the single exception is the Bedrock-LLM OCR cell on manylists_400, which split one statement in two. Agentic cost is still run-to-run variable on a fixed document and must be budgeted as a range rather than a mean; §4 measures that with repeats.
  8. Bedrock-LLM OCR is still the only backend that gets values wrong, and the evidence for that is its scalar fields rather than its rows. scalar_accuracy compares the two top-level scalar fields by label, so it does not key on the row identifier this backend perturbs and is unaffected by the measurement defect below: this cell gets 7 of its 14 scalar fields right, where every other cell gets 13 or 14 of 14 — and one of the two fields is Account Number, a fixed-width identifier. On rows, it is the only cell of the nineteen with any cell accuracy below 1.000, measured at 0.990 over 800 cells_compared on manylists_400, the one affected document whose identifier join is bijective (dups 0, n_gaps 0, a full 400-row prefix). It is also the cheapest cell in the grid. See §2, finding 4. ⚠️ No recall figure and no other cell-accuracy figure is quoted for this cell, because they are not measurements. Its apparent row loss is #1500: rows_extracted counted distinct recognisable identifiers, this backend duplicates a leading zero, and ids collapsed onto one another with no row lost. Three of this cell’s runs carry that signature exactly — dups 38 with n_gaps 38, the arithmetic of a one-to-one collapse — while the identifier-free conf_rows_expected and conf_rows_scored read the full truth count on all three. The same identifier keys the cell-accuracy join, so the figures computed over those shrunken denominators are artefacts of the join rather than value errors.
  9. The cheap end completes everything and returns half the rows. Neither Nova model can finish the agentic path — 247 invalid sequence as part of ToolUse stream errors in one grid, each retried as transient (#895) — so both ran the smaller simplegrid matrix instead, where they completed 63 of 63 runs with no error at mean recall 0.4809 (Nova Pro) and 0.4690 (Nova Lite). Nova Pro’s 0.9361 cell accuracy is over 6,960 cells against the same matrix’s Sonnet 4.6 control at 39,462 — “94% accurate on the rows it chose to return”, not 94% accurate. Their own confidence does not catch it either: 0.007% and 0.707% of leaves fall below 0.9 (§5.1).

What changed between releases is tracked separately in the Release Audit Trail — this paper focuses on choosing a configuration at the current release.

1. Methodology (summary)

Section titled “1. Methodology (summary)”

See benchmarks/matrices/METHODOLOGY.md for the full protocol. In brief:

  • Synthetic corpus (exact GT): generated bank statements whose every transaction row carries a unique SEQnnnnn tag, so completeness and accuracy are measured exactly, and size, row width, list count, text length and OCR noise are controlled variables.
  • Reference corpora (real, labelled): RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, one class) and OmniAI-OCR-Benchmark (20 documents, nine classes), run as Test Studio test sets and scored by the product’s own evaluation against their labels. Both were run for this release (core suite), at the shipped default model and at the control model.
  • Config matrix: 19 curated core cells — the OCR × mode × assessment decision space plus the v0.7 feature arms (enforcement, forcing, schema restatement, section splitting) — a two-cell scaling series, a repeated-measures cost suite, the two hazard re-verification suites (intconf, advverify), the feature A/B suites for every knob this release touched, and the model sweeps. Control arms live in a separate control_cells: block and are excluded from core_cells.
  • Scoring is resolver-free (reads S3 + DynamoDB metering directly); costs priced from pricing.yaml; calibration from explainability_info confidence leaves.

⚠️ Classification granularity, modality and strategy are not measured in this edition. Granularity (page | packet), each call’s modality.text / .images and classification.strategy (combined | split_then_classify) shipped after this edition’s runs, and no cell here varies them — every classification cell above is granularity: page with text and images, combined, which is the shipped default. So this page recommends a classification model and says nothing about which granularity, modality or strategy to pick.

The guidance that does exist for them, in Classification, is reasoned rather than measured: the token arithmetic is sound — a page-level call repeats the instructions, class list, descriptions and few-shot examples once per page while a packet call pays them once, and image tokens are identical either way — but nothing has measured the accuracy side on a corpus. Read “packet + images off is the large cost lever” as a cost claim derived from mechanism, and read nothing into it about accuracy.

benchmarks/harness/run_classification_bench.py --variants is the axis for closing this, and #1513 tracks running it. How simple mode behaves as documents grow

Section titled “How simple mode behaves as documents grow”

The simple-mode column above is not a gradient. It is three distinct behaviours with two sharp boundaries, and they want different responses from a deployment:

  1. 25 → 800 rows / 1 → 17 pages: it completes, with everything. COMPLETED, recall 1.000, cell accuracy 1.000 on denominators from 50 to 1,600 cells. Nothing to manage.
  2. ⚠️ 1,200 and 1,600 rows / 25 and 33 pages: the extraction succeeds and the document fails. Both runs report FAILED, and both report recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 over 2,400 and 3,200 cells_compared — every row, every value. The extraction was not truncated and was not wrong: it was validated clean (validation_valid_rate 1.000, 0 errors), the confidence pass scored every row (conf_rows_expected = conf_rows_scored = 1,200 and 1,600, conf_coverage 1.000), and then the document failed anyway. What fails is AssessmentStep exhausting its Lambda (#1488, fixed by PR #1493) — the same cause that cost eleven cells across seven arms their completion in §5.1 — and the wall-clock is what gives it away: 2,029 s and 2,088 s against 477 s at 800 rows, which is repeated Lambda lifetimes rather than a longer request. A complete, validated, persisted extraction was being discarded.
  3. 3,200 rows / 66 pages: refused before extraction inference, which is correct. FAILED in 56 s having returned 0 rows and 0 sections, with no extraction tokens billed at all — the spend is the OCR and classification that had already run. The document is beyond what one request with 66 page images can carry; benchmarks/matrices/config_matrix.yaml records this size as an expected ExtractionInputTooLarge rejection rather than a regression, and §5.2’s astracap reproduces it 4 times over, twice on Sonnet 5 and twice on GPT-6 Astra, at 50–55 s and 0 rows each. Refusing is the right outcome: nothing is attempted and nothing wrong is returned.

⚠️ So completeness_recall is the wrong signal in regime 2 and status is the right one — and in regime 3 it is the other way round. A reader who ranks by recall concludes simple mode is flawless at 1,600 rows; a reader who ranks by completion rate concludes it returned nothing there. Both are wrong. Pair the two columns, which is why the table above prints status first.

⚠️ No per-page input-token budget is published for the sizes that failed, and the reason is that this suite cannot measure them. A failed document bills no extraction tokens at all, so for the three sizes above 800 rows there is no record of the request that was refused or of the one whose result was discarded. What the suite does measure is the simple-mode extraction input on the four sizes that completed — 2,552, 7,958, 26,616 and 51,433 tokens at 1, 3, 9 and 17 pages, about 3,100 tokens per page over the 3–17-page span, with the 1-page point below that line because fixed overhead dominates it. That figure describes the completing regime only. Extrapolating it to 25, 33 or 66 pages would produce a number this suite does not support, so none is given — and for regime 2 it would answer the wrong question anyway, since those requests succeeded.

The product mechanism behind regime 3 is unchanged and is documented rather than measured here. Simple mode’s pre-flight estimate logs and sends; what refuses an oversized request is Bedrock, on the size of the request its page images make, and #994 relieved it by clamping every image in a many-image request to 2,000 px per side. Two separate Bedrock limits bind: a stricter per-image dimension cap once a request carries more than 20 image blocks, and a cap on the total request payload. It is the payload limit that produces ExtractionInputTooLarge, because Bedrock reports an oversized payload with the same “Input is too long for requested model” wording it uses for a context overflow; the per-image dimension rejection has its own distinct wording and is matched separately (see the note in idp_common/utils/bedrock_utils.py). The clamp cuts pixel area, so it relieves both.

The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)

Section titled “The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)”

extraction.row_shortfall_action is the knob that lets an observed row shortfall decide the outcome, added at 0.6.10 for the truncated-but-COMPLETED shape — a run that returns a fraction of a list and reports success, which §5.1 measures on three extraction models at 17 pages. Nothing in this suite exercises it: no run here lost a row, so extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate never fired, and the two documents that failed failed in the confidence pass with a complete extraction behind them — which is a different defect (regime 2 above) and one no row-count check can see. The knob is described here because it is the control a reader reaches for after reading this table, not because this table measures it.

The extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate detection is unchanged — the rows extracted against the rows in the section’s OCR tables of the same width, a floor of 30 and a “fewer than half” ratio — and under fail the partial rows and the diagnosis are written before the section fails, so a truncated run reports FAILED with a message naming the rows extracted, the OCR estimate and the remedy. §2’s grid and §5.1’s per-model grid are where truncated runs are actually observed on this release.

The default is warn. The reason is the check’s evidence, not caution: matched tables are summed over the whole section on width alone, and a 2- or 3-property array modelling an entity group is structurally identical to one modelling table rows. On the default preset a completely correct extraction of Bank-Statement.account_summary (2 properties, 5 rows) scores 0.13, because a monthly statement’s 31-row two-column Daily Balance table is counted as evidence about it. Nine such fields ship in the config library, so fail as a default would fail correct extractions. Extraction and confidence lists the shapes to check before turning it on.

Two notes on the threshold. It is not a new number: the failure fires exactly where the warning already fired, so no second threshold was chosen to make these cells come out right. And across the 3,631 recorded benchmark runs that reach the check’s population, non-zero recall is strongly bimodal — 65 runs below 0.5, 3,368 at ~1.000, and one single run anywhere in [0.3, 0.5) — so the outcome is insensitive to the ratio’s exact value within that band. Of those 65, three are Advanced-mode runs (of 1,231 Advanced runs in the population) and all three lost real rows, though only one lost them as a clean stop-early cut: the other two have truncation_prefix = 0, meaning the loss is scattered through the list rather than a prefix. What none of this establishes is the false-failure rate against real corpora: the 65 are all runs that genuinely lost more than half their rows, and the group-shaped-array problem above was found by reading the shipped schemas, not measured on a document set.

Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it

Section titled “Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it”

On v0.6.7 simple mode reported “complete” at 1,200 rows and recovered 0.72–0.79 of the rows at 3,200 — and that completeness was an artefact of a different behaviour: the classifier split every large document into 6–18 sections, each small enough to extract, so the rows arrived as N per-section lists that a consumer had to reassemble. With #726/#817 every document is one section and that scaffolding is gone, which is why the shape above looks nothing like it. Three things follow, each measured in the table above:

  1. The simple-mode envelope on this corpus is 800 rows / 17 pages, and the 800-row point is the one to hold loosely. It completes with all 800 rows here, in §2’s grid run, and in all 19 of §2’s cells — but it is one draw per cell, and §5.1 measures the same 17-page document in simple mode returning ~351 to ~454 of 800 rows on three of seven extraction models while reporting COMPLETED, with cell accuracy 0.9985–1.0000 computed on the rows they returned. The single long response is what varies, and which side of the limit a run lands on moves with the model and with the input text. Treat ~400 rows / ~10 pages as the comfortable simple-mode envelope and 800 as the edge of it; use advanced mode above that.
  2. Above the envelope, simple mode does not degrade gracefully — it stops being available. At 1,200 and 1,600 rows the extraction is perfect and the document fails (regime 2); at 3,200 rows the request is refused outright (regime 3). Neither outcome gives a consumer a partial list to work with, and neither is improved by tuning: the remedy at every size above the envelope is advanced mode, which returns 1.000 at all seven.
  3. ⚠️ A status column and a recall column disagree here, and which one lies depends on the size. This is the one part of §3 to carry away. COMPLETED is not evidence of completeness — §5.1’s truncated-but-completed runs are the proof — and FAILED is not evidence of lost rows, because at 1,200 and 1,600 rows every row was extracted, validated and scored before the document failed. Read status and completeness_recall and cells_compared together, or the same table supports two opposite conclusions.

Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow

Section titled “Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow”

Advanced (agentic sharding) holds recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 from 25 through 3,200 rows / 66 pages at the control model, on denominators from 50 to 6,400 cells_compared, one section per document throughout — and, in the premium study (§5.2), at Sonnet 5, Opus 5 and GPT-6 Astra too. Sharding keeps each call small, so neither the single-response truncation nor an input-context limit is reached.

The practical limits are cost and wall-clock, and at the control model both stay modest: $4.9910 and 509 s at 3,200 rows, against $0.1439 and 40 s at 25 rows. Cost is close to linear in rows from 400 upward — $0.00144, $0.00171, $0.00158, $0.00160 and $0.00156 per row at 400, 800, 1,200, 1,600 and 3,200 rows — so there is no cost cliff at the top of this series; the 25-row point is the outlier, at $0.00576 per row, because the fixed per-document overhead dominates a one-page document. For the shipped default at the 3,200-row size, §5.2’s astracap measures Sonnet 5 at $5.7954 and $5.8167 over two repeats, 411 s and 406 s, so the control is modestly the cheaper and modestly the slower of the two there.

The confidence pass converges at 800 rows and is what fails above it. At 800 rows the Nova Lite batch ceiling and per-batch budget shipped in #861 (§7) do their job: the simple-mode run reports assessment_recovered_with_retries with 8 rows recovered, 1 truncated call, 1 split, 0 unrecoverable rows, on a token-aware batch of 12 against a configured 25 — a ladder that converges rather than one that does not. That is the v0.6.7 aborted run (simple @800: extraction complete, then five 900-second Assessment timeouts) closed. At 1,200 and 1,600 rows the confidence pass is nonetheless where the document is lost — #1488, regime 2 above — and the shape is different: it scores every row successfully and then exhausts the Lambda, so there is no retry ladder to tune and no unrecovered row to report. #861 fixed the ladder; #1493 is the fix for this.

Turn it off for shard headroom, not for dollars. The input saving is genuine but cheap: it is almost entirely cache reads, so 9,181 fewer input-side tokens is worth about $0.007 — while 2,347 more output tokens cost about $0.039. Those two figures reconcile the measured extraction-cost difference (+$0.0319/doc) to the cent. The release grid in §2b reaches the same conclusion independently, on a different corpus and a different edition: restate-off $1.548 against restate-on $1.523, the same direction and a comparable per-document size. Two grids agreeing on the sign is the reason to trust it, given neither reaches significance on cost alone. Since #775 the reclaimed tokens are real shard budget, so headroom is the reason to use this knob; whether it moves a shard count still depends on the document sitting near a boundary.

⚠️ The output-token increase is the part to watch, and it is bimodal rather than uniform. On manylists_400 every restate-on run emitted 13,406–13,590 output tokens, while five of fifteen restate-off runs emitted 20,201–20,368 — a mode the on arm never entered (Fisher p = 0.042). The remaining ten sat only 100–250 tokens above the on arm. So the risk of turning it off is not a slightly chattier agent every time; it is a roughly one-in-three chance of a run that emits half again as much. Measured on one model and one document shape.
