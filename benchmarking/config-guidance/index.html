Abstract

Section titled “Abstract”

We benchmark the GenAI IDP accelerator across a controlled matrix of configuration options (OCR backend, extraction mode, assessment mode, the feature-option arms, the extraction, classification and confidence models, and the classification granularity, modality and strategy) and document types and sizes (synthetic documents with exact ground truth, plus three real labelled corpora). We quantify seven dimensions per configuration: success/failure, list completeness, per-row field accuracy, confidence calibration, latency, token use, and cost.

Headline results at v0.6.14:

  1. Both Opus models are at ceiling on the whole extraction grid for the second edition running, and Opus 5.5 is still the cheaper of the two. On the 19-cell × 7-document grid Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Opus 5 each completed 133 of 133 runs at recall 1.0000 and cell accuracy 1.0000 over 83,790 cells_compared, the grid’s full denominator — for $105.95 against $127.08, 17% less for the same result, a ratio that can be quoted because both are flat-rate. GPT-6 Astra, the most expensive arm, is at recall 0.9924 and the lowest cell accuracy of the grid, 0.9810 over 82,184 cells, for $191.85, which is a lower bound — so Opus 5.5 is at least 45% less than Astra and more accurate (§5.1, §5.5).
  2. The shipped default still truncates the 17-page document in simple mode, and still reports success. Sonnet 5’s grid recall is 0.9463 over 72,364 cells, and all of the shortfall is one document: on the 800-row large_narrow in simple mode it returned all 800 rows on 5 of 13 runs and 43 to 190 rows on the other 8, each COMPLETED and each flagged extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate. In advanced mode the same model returned 800 of 800 on 6 of 6. The rule from the last three editions holds: use advanced mode above ~400 rows (§5.5, §3).
  3. The confidence pass now covers most of a large simple-mode list. In the scaling study the 1,200- and 1,600-row simple-mode documents complete with every row at cell accuracy 1.000 and 95% and 80% of rows confidence-scored, against 33–38% and 19–23% in the v0.6.13 edition — one draw per size here, so read it as a direction. The same movement shows at the 3,200-row ceiling, where Sonnet 5’s wide-shard confidence coverage went from 22–78% to 97–99% (§5.2), and every one of the 931 runs of the seven-arm extraction grid completed, against two AssessmentStep memory kills there at v0.6.13 (#1678). Advanced mode holds recall 1.000 and full confidence coverage at every size from 25 to 3,200 rows (§3).
  4. Both classification-variant failure modes are closed, and packet granularity’s boundary advantage is smaller than first measured. Every image-bearing packet variant now classifies the 18–20-page DocSplit packets it failed at v0.6.13 (#1676), and text-only page granularity no longer fails a page with no text (#1677). Over the 19 packets every variant scored, the packet-level variants place section boundaries at 0.600–0.710 against the page-level default’s 0.509, at 29–59% of its classification cost per page; the v0.6.13 edition measured 0.711–0.821 against 0.446. Twenty documents, one run each, so read this as direction rather than ranking (§5.6).
  5. On a real labelled corpus simple mode is still at least as accurate as advanced at half the cost, by a margin now inside the noise. On RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, all 19 cells, scored by the product’s own evaluation) the matched pair scores 0.798 simple against 0.774 advanced weighted accuracy, at $0.1428 against $0.2825 per document — but four cells running the identical simple configuration span 0.771–0.799, so the accuracy gap is one draw’s noise and the cost gap is the finding. sectionSplitting: disabled is the best cell for the third edition running, at 0.836 (§2c).
  6. 🚨 Claude Sonnet 5.5 still loses most of a 400-row list (#1494). 15 of its 133 grid runs return between 2 and 20 rows of 400, every one in simple mode on a 400-row document. Per-document recall is 0.7413 on med_narrow, 0.8974 on wide_400 and 0.5947 on manylists_400, against 1.0000 on every other document. 13 of the 15 now carry a completeness issue; the two that do not are 10-row runs on the 8-column wide_400, where there is no exact-width OCR table to check a row count against (§5.1).
  7. Neither premium knob on the other two model axes earns its price. Upgrading the classification model buys nothing measurable: every arm produces downstream recall 1.0000 over the same 83,790 cells, and each arm gets 132–133 of 133 boundaries right; the grid bill rises from $89.93 to $106.11 under Sonnet 5. Sonnet 5 as the confidence model is a far more conservative scorer, not a more accurate one: about 638 of 119,960 confidence leaves below 0.9 against the default’s 0 of 119,937, for $160.70 against $90.33, with identical cell accuracy (0.9997 over 83,790 cells in all three arms) (§5.3, §5.4).
  8. Both standing list-loss hazards stay closed, and every §2 run produced one section. integrated confidence with simple extraction is recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 on 4 of 4 repeats, at the same cost to the cent as separate, and the advanced-mode tool-decline list loss is 0 of 8 at the shipped default (§2.1). All 133 grid runs produced exactly one section (§2).
  9. Bedrock-LLM OCR is still the one backend that gets values wrong. It returns every row — recall 1.000 over 4,410 cells — at cell accuracy 0.990, the only cell of the nineteen below 1.000, with 10 of 14 scalar fields right where every other cell gets 12 to 14, and the grid’s only validation error. It is also the cheapest cell, at $0.3821 per document. Do not use it where identifiers or amounts matter (§2, finding 4).
  10. The cheap end completes everything and returns half the rows. Neither Nova model can finish the agentic path (#895), so both ran the smaller simplegrid matrix, where they completed 63 of 63 runs at mean recall 0.5035 (Nova Pro) and 0.4980 (Nova Lite). Nova Pro’s 0.9225 cell accuracy is over 7,858 cells against the same matrix’s Sonnet 4.6 control at 39,690 (§5.1).

What changed between releases is tracked separately in the Release Audit Trail — this paper focuses on choosing a configuration at the current release.

1. Methodology (summary)

Section titled “1. Methodology (summary)”

See benchmarks/matrices/METHODOLOGY.md for the full protocol. In brief:

  • Synthetic corpus (exact GT): generated bank statements whose every transaction row carries a unique SEQnnnnn tag, so completeness and accuracy are measured exactly, and size, row width, list count, text length and OCR noise are controlled variables.
  • Reference corpora (real, labelled): RealKIE-FCC-Verified (20 documents, one class) and OmniAI-OCR-Benchmark (20 documents, nine classes), run as Test Studio test sets and scored by the product’s own evaluation against their labels. Both were run for this release (core suite), at the control model only.
  • Classification corpus (real, labelled per page): the first 20 packets of DocSplit-Poly-Seq (RVL-CDIP-derived, 13 classes, 298 pages), run through benchmarks/harness/run_classification_bench.py and scored per page and per section against its labels (§5.6).
  • Config matrix: 19 curated core cells — the OCR × mode × assessment decision space plus the feature-option arms (enforcement, forcing, schema restatement, section splitting) — a two-cell scaling series, a repeated-measures cost suite, the two hazard re-verification suites (intconf, advverify), the feature A/B suites for the knobs named in the scope table, and the model sweeps. Control arms live in a separate control_cells: block and are excluded from core_cells.
  • Scoring is resolver-free (reads S3 + DynamoDB metering directly); costs priced from pricing.yaml; calibration from explainability_info confidence leaves.

How simple mode behaves as documents grow

Section titled “How simple mode behaves as documents grow”

The simple-mode column above is not a gradient. It is three distinct behaviours with two sharp boundaries, and they want different responses from a deployment:

  1. 25 → 800 rows / 1 → 17 pages: it completes, with everything. COMPLETED, recall 1.000, cell accuracy 1.000, every row confidence-scored. At 800 rows the confidence pass is already the larger part of the bill — $0.67 of $1.62 per document, against $0.04 for the same pass in advanced mode — and of the wall-clock: 555 s against 209 s at 400 rows. The batch-shrinking ladder converges there (assessment_recovered_with_retries).
  2. ⚠️ 1,200 and 1,600 rows / 25 and 33 pages: every row is extracted and returned, and some of them have no confidence. Both runs report COMPLETED, recall 1.000 and cell accuracy 1.000 over 2,400 and 3,200 cells_compared, validated clean. And both carry assessment_incomplete: the confidence pass scored 1,138 of 1,200 rows and 1,276 of 1,600, then stopped to stay inside the Lambda’s time budget, so the remaining 62 and 324 rows come back with confidence: null and a per-row reason naming the time budget (#1488). That is a large move from the v0.6.13 edition, where the same pass scored only 33–38% of the rows at 1,200 and 19–23% at 1,600 over three draws each: 95% and 80% coverage on this release. One draw per size here, so read it as a direction until it is repeated. It is consistent with §5.2, where Sonnet 5’s agentic adv-wide cell on 3,200 rows moved from 22–78% confidence coverage to 97–99% between the two releases, and with this release’s confidence-step memory fix (#1678), but the run records no per-batch timings, so that attribution is an inference. A consumer that gates review on confidence still has to handle the unscored rows, because an unscored row raises no confidence alert.
  3. 3,200 rows / 66 pages: refused before extraction inference, which is correct. FAILED in about 47 s having returned 0 rows and 0 sections, with no extraction tokens billed at all — the spend is the OCR and classification that had already run. The request is too long for one call; benchmarks/matrices/config_matrix.yaml records this size as an expected rejection rather than a regression, and §5.2’s astracap reproduces it 4 times over, twice on Sonnet 5 and twice on GPT-6 Astra. Refusing is the right outcome: nothing is attempted and nothing wrong is returned.

⚠️ So in regime 2 neither status nor completeness_recall tells you what is missing — conf_coverage and the assessment_incomplete issue do. A reader who ranks by recall or by completion rate concludes simple mode is flawless at 1,600 rows, and on the extraction it is. What is missing is the confidence on one row in five at 1,600.

⚠️ No per-page input-token budget is published for the refused size. A refused document bills no extraction tokens, so there is no record of the request. What the suite does measure is the simple-mode extraction input on the six sizes that completed — 2,552, 7,958, 26,616, 51,433, 76,482 and 101,733 tokens at 1, 3, 9, 17, 25 and 33 pages, identical to the v0.6.13 edition to the token and about 3,100 tokens per page over the 3–33-page span, with the 1-page point below that line because fixed overhead dominates it. Extrapolating it to 66 pages would produce a number this suite does not support, so none is given.

The product mechanism behind regime 3 is unchanged and is documented rather than measured here. Simple mode’s pre-flight estimate logs and sends; what refuses an oversized request is Bedrock, on the size of the request its page images make, and #994 relieved it by clamping every image in a many-image request to 2,000 px per side. Two separate Bedrock limits bind: a stricter per-image dimension cap once a request carries more than 20 image blocks, and a cap on the total request payload. It is the payload limit that produces ExtractionInputTooLarge, because Bedrock reports an oversized payload with the same “Input is too long for requested model” wording it uses for a context overflow; the per-image dimension rejection has its own distinct wording and is matched separately (see the note in idp_common/utils/bedrock_utils.py). The clamp cuts pixel area, so it relieves both.

The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)

Section titled “The row-shortfall setting (not exercised by this table)”

extraction.row_shortfall_action is the knob that lets an observed row shortfall decide the outcome, added at 0.6.10 for the truncated-but-COMPLETED shape — a run that returns a fraction of a list and reports success, which §5.1 measures on four extraction models at 17 pages and on Sonnet 5.5 at 9. Nothing in this suite exercises it: no run here lost a row, so extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate never fired. The knob is described here because it is the control a reader reaches for after reading about truncation, not because this table measures it.

The extraction_rows_below_ocr_estimate detection is unchanged — the rows extracted against the rows in the section’s OCR tables of the same width, a floor of 30 and a “fewer than half” ratio — and under fail the partial rows and the diagnosis are written before the section fails, so a truncated run reports FAILED with a message naming the rows extracted, the OCR estimate and the remedy. Since v0.6.13 the case where that check cannot run is reported too: when the matched evidence is under 30 rows while 30 or more rows came back, the section carries extraction_row_count_unverified (info) instead of silence (#1489). It reports the absence of evidence and never a shortfall. ⚠️ Its floor cuts both ways: a list truncated to fewer than 30 rows on a document without exact-width tables is reported by neither issue, which is exactly the shape of two of Sonnet 5.5’s 10-row runs on wide_400 in §5.1.

The default is warn. The reason is the check’s evidence, not caution: matched tables are summed over the whole section on width alone, and a 2- or 3-property array modelling an entity group is structurally identical to one modelling table rows. On the default preset a completely correct extraction of Bank-Statement.account_summary (2 properties, 5 rows) scores 0.13, because a monthly statement’s 31-row two-column Daily Balance table is counted as evidence about it. Nine such fields ship in the config library, so fail as a default would fail correct extractions. Extraction and confidence lists the shapes to check before turning it on.

Two notes on the threshold. It is not a new number: the failure fires exactly where the warning already fired, so no second threshold was chosen to make these cells come out right. And across the 3,631 recorded benchmark runs that reach the check’s population, non-zero recall is strongly bimodal — 65 runs below 0.5, 3,368 at ~1.000, and one single run anywhere in [0.3, 0.5) — so the outcome is insensitive to the ratio’s exact value within that band. Of those 65, three are Advanced-mode runs (of 1,231 Advanced runs in the population) and all three lost real rows, though only one lost them as a clean stop-early cut: the other two have truncation_prefix = 0, meaning the loss is scattered through the list rather than a prefix. What none of this establishes is the false-failure rate against real corpora: the 65 are all runs that genuinely lost more than half their rows, and the group-shaped-array problem above was found by reading the shipped schemas, not measured on a document set.

Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it

Section titled “Simple-mode size limit, and what to do beyond it”

On v0.6.7 simple mode reported “complete” at 1,200 rows and recovered 0.72–0.79 of the rows at 3,200 — and that completeness was an artefact of a different behaviour: the classifier split every large document into 6–18 sections, each small enough to extract. With #726/#817 every document is one section and that scaffolding is gone. Three things follow:

  1. The simple-mode envelope for a complete result on this corpus is 800 rows / 17 pages, and the 800-row point is the one to hold loosely. At the control model it completes with all 800 rows and full confidence here and on all 19 of §2’s cells — but §5.1 measures the same 17-page document in simple mode returning between 0 and 190 of 800 rows on some runs of four of the seven extraction models while reporting COMPLETED, the shipped default among them (8 of its 13 runs). The single long response is what varies, and which side of the limit a run lands on moves with the model and with the input text. Treat ~400 rows / ~9 pages as the comfortable simple-mode envelope and 800 as the edge of it; use advanced mode above that.
  2. Above the envelope, simple mode returns the rows without all of the confidence, then stops being available. At 1,200 and 1,600 rows the extraction is complete and 5% and 20% of the rows are unscored on this release’s single draw (regime 2); at 3,200 rows the request is refused outright (regime 3). Neither is improved by tuning the extraction: the remedy at every size above the envelope is advanced mode, which returns every row with confidence at all seven sizes. The assessment_incomplete message names two other remedies — more Lambda time, or less work per section — and on this evidence they treat the confidence half only.
  3. ⚠️ No single column summarises a simple-mode run at size. COMPLETED is not evidence of completeness — §5.1’s truncated-but-completed runs are the proof — and recall 1.000 is not evidence that the document is review-ready, because at 1,200 and 1,600 rows some rows are unscored. Read status, completeness_recall, cells_compared and conf_coverage together.

Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow

Section titled “Advanced mode: cost and processing time as documents grow”

Advanced (agentic sharding) holds recall 1.000, cell accuracy 1.000 and full confidence coverage from 25 through 3,200 rows / 66 pages at the control model, on 7 of 7 runs, on denominators from 50 to 6,400 cells_compared per run — and, in the premium study (§5.2), at Sonnet 5 and GPT-6 Astra too.

The practical limits are cost and wall-clock, and at the control model both stay modest: $5.0135 and 374 s at 3,200 rows (one draw; the v0.6.13 edition’s three draws ranged $4.39–4.99 and 465–747 s), against $0.0958 and 37 s at 25 rows. Two values this cell does not cover. sectionSplitting has a third value, page, which asserts one document per page; it is not in this matrix. Like disabled it is an assertion rather than a preference, and it is the right answer only for a corpus of single-page documents. Granularity is a separate axis from this cell and is measured in §5.6 on a labelled packet corpus, where packet granularity placed boundaries correctly more often than page; see also Classification.

Turn it off for shard headroom, not for dollars. The input saving is genuine but cheap: it is almost entirely cache reads, so 9,181 fewer input-side tokens is worth about $0.007 — while 2,347 more output tokens cost about $0.039. Those two figures reconcile the measured extraction-cost difference (+$0.0319/doc) to the cent. ⚠️ The release grids do not settle the sign. At one draw per document they put restate-off above restate-on on this release ($0.6343 against $0.6136, +3.4%) and below it in the two before — $0.6164 against $0.6188 at v0.6.13, $0.6142 against $0.6292 at v0.6.12 — three differences of 0.4–3.4% in alternating directions, all inside the agentic spread §4 measures. So the cost claim rests on the dedicated A/B alone, which itself does not reach significance on cost. Since #775 the reclaimed tokens are real shard budget, so headroom is the reason to use this knob; whether it moves a shard count still depends on the document sitting near a boundary.

⚠️ The output-token increase is the part to watch, and it is bimodal rather than uniform. The remaining ten sat only 100–250 tokens above the on arm. So the risk of turning it off is not a slightly chattier agent every time; it is a roughly one-in-three chance of a run that emits half again as much. Measured on one model and one document shape.
